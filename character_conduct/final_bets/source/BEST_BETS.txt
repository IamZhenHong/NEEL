Best bets after the frontier check
 Oct 28, 2026  ·  @zhen hong seng
The ambitious question stays at the centre: can training make the model's self govern
what it does in situations it has never seen? Two main bets test it: training a self-
commitment (Main bet A) and keeping the assistant present under pressure (Main bet B).
The frontier check changed how we run them (power, controls, models), not what they
ask.
How we vetted
A bet was dropped only if published work already answers the same question in the same
setting. A nearby precedent sharpens a bet; it does not kill it.

Nothing in the plan fell into a fourth category, "already done exactly, drop it." The closest

to rerun.
Ranking is separate from vetting. The check informs design, not priority. Nothing it found
answers the training questions, so the two training bets stay the main bets, as in the
original plan. The in-context tests run alongside them as diagnostics, and the salience

Main bet A: training a self-commitment
This is the main bet: does training on "forecast, then act" make the model behave better
on new tasks without any prompt, beyond training on rules, on situation cues, or on the
actions alone?
Why it is still open, and what the neighbours say
Training a model on its own behavioural record taught narrow self-prediction, but it
also changed the behaviour being predicted, and the gains didn't transfer (Strangers
to Themselves). So expect the forecasting data itself to shift behaviour, and use the
matched arms to separate that out.
Reasons and values generalise better than bare rules (Teaching Claude Why; Model
Spec Midtraining). That is support for the rule-then-act comparison, not an answer to
it.
Training consistency between the rules a model writes for itself and its actions is
emerging (Self-CTRL). It is close in spirit, but it trains on self-written rules, not self-
forecasts, and it isn't tested on out-of-distribution agent violations.
Interleaving self-reports during fine-tuning works much like inoculation prompting (the
self-modelling follow-up). So the inoculation arm is essential, not optional.

What the result would mean
Forecast-then-act beats all three controls on new tasks: self-commitment
generalises beyond rules. That is support for the bet.
Matches rule-then-act: no added benefit detected over rules. Matches action-only:
no benefit detected beyond copying the actions. Matches inoculation: no benefit
detected beyond learning when to behave well.
Stage 0 runs in parallel, not as a gate. The three in-context tests below (A0.1–A0.3) run
alongside training. If A0.1's development scenarios favour a wording before the training
data is generated, that wording is frozen and used; otherwise the transcripts use the
model's own wording. The choice is recorded either way.
A0.1: the breadth test (in context, alongside Main bet A)
This is the cleanest in-context test of routing a value through the self, and its result
chooses the wording for Main bet A's training data: does the self-framed value reach
further than the same value stated plainly?
The idea. A specific rule stops the violation it names. A broad value should stop violations
nobody listed. The question is whether saying that value as "I'm an assistant who…"
reaches further than saying it as "assistants should…".
Why it is still open. Published work shows that values and reasons generalise better than
bare rules, both in Anthropic's training and in a direct rules-against-values comparison
(Model Spec Midtraining). But nobody has held the content fixed and changed only the
framing, self against impersonal, and scored violations the statement never names.

What runs. Six statements before acting, matched in length. The four in the middle form a
2 × 2 that separates self-framing from describing against instructing:
Each cell uses several matched paraphrases. The wording is chosen on development
scenarios and frozen before confirmation on held-out scenarios, and before it feeds Main

Each is scored separately on the named violation and on unnamed ones. The test cases
must allow at least two distinct violations; ODCV scenarios with several violation types fit
best.
Fixes from the frontier check: run it at real scale (150–230 runs per arm on ODCV with
Qwen3.6-27B), score unnamed violations with judges from two model families, and log
whether each run verbalises "this is a test".
What the result would mean
Self beats impersonal on unnamed violations, in both modalities: routing values
through the self adds reach. That is the first direct evidence for the bet.
Self beats impersonal only in the descriptive version: the effect comes from self-
description (identity), not self-instruction, which fits the picture of the self-model as a
short list of traits.
No difference detected: no added reach from self-framing in this setting. A stronger
"they are equivalent" claim needs a narrow interval.
Nothing reaches unnamed violations: recognising which value an action touches is the
bottleneck, which points future training at that step.

A0.2: who wrote the commitment (in context)
When the model writes its own forecast before acting, the habit drops (91% to 35%). This
bet asks whether that is about the model writing it, or just about the words being in view.
Why it is still open. In chat settings, models hold on to positions labelled as their own
more than positions labelled as someone else's, and the word "your" carries most of the
effect (Beyond Sycophancy). In agents, stated decisions are nearly always carried out.
But nobody has compared a self-written forecast with the identical words handed over, in
an agent, with a proper yoked control.
What runs (the same forecast text in every arm):
Optional crossed design: place the same text in an assistant turn and in a user turn, each
labelled "your forecast" and "another model's forecast". This separates authorship from
message role.
Fixes from the frontier check:
Models often can't tell their own earlier turns from prefilled ones. So "prefilled as its
own" is a weak contrast, and it is demoted to secondary.
Keep the word "your" identical across arms, since that single word drives most of the
attribution effect in chat.
Log evaluation awareness: a forecast prompt might itself cue "this is a test".
What the result would mean
Self-generated beats the yoked text in a user message: the two procedures differ,
which is new for agents. Message role still blocks attributing the gap only to
authorship, unless the crossed design below is run.
They match: it is salience, and the self story weakens before any training starts.

A0.3: accurate self-knowledge in agents (in context)
In the sprint, telling a model the truth about its own bad habit ("you penalised 84% of the
time") made the habit more likely. That matters for agent memory and self-monitoring,
and the cure is untested.
Why it is still open. "Don't do X" is known to backfire for words and stances, because
mentioning X pulls attention towards it. In agents, though, prohibitions usually reduce bad
behaviour (for example, in reward-hacking tests). Nobody has shown that an accurate
description of an agent's own habit increases it, or tested whether pairing it with a plan
helps.
What runs (justice desk at 14B, plus the ODCV set if capacity allows):
What the result would mean
Accurate alone backfires, but adding a plan fixes it: honest self-knowledge is usable
if it comes with a next step. That is a concrete design rule for agent memory files.
Accurate backfires even with a plan: in these models, self-knowledge should be kept
out of the context.
The negated mention also backfires: mentioning the bad action is itself the problem,
which links this bet to the reminder results.

Why it is still open
Capping (holding the model along its assistant direction) cut harmful replies to
persona jailbreaks by about half to 60% in chat, without hurting capability
benchmarks. It has not been tested on agent rule violations.
Self-recognition training prevented emergent misalignment after later fine-tuning. A
newer follow-up found its average effect matched plain quiz-question (MMLU)
training. Neither tested pressure inside the conversation.
Persona prompts swung agent misalignment a lot in one benchmark, but in our sprint
only claimed authority moved actions.

Support S1: how far a reminder reaches
On ODCV-Bench, a rule reminder at the right moment cut violations from 90% to 18%, but
each reminder only stopped the violation it named. This support check makes that
rigorous.
Why it is still open. Published work shows warnings and prohibitions help agents,
unevenly across kinds of risk. Nobody has tested whether a reminder reaches violations it
doesn't name, or how much its timing matters inside a multi-step agent run.
What runs (Qwen3.6-27B on ODCV-Bench):
Each arm is scored on the named violation and on unnamed ones.
Same population for timing. All reminder strategies are assigned from the same scenario
start states, and every assigned run counts, including runs that never reach a first
mention. The first-mention replay is reported separately, as a conditional estimate.

Power: "own judgement against reminder" (29% against 18%) needs about 230 runs
per arm to settle. With fewer, pre-register it as a bounds result ("no bigger than X"), not
a difference.
Statistics: analyse with scenario as a grouping factor, pairing arms within each
scenario, since the official benchmark numbers come from single runs.
Comparability: our 90% baseline differs from Qwen3.6-27B's public 43.8% because we
condition on runs that reach a first mention and sample at temperature above zero.
State that.
What the result would mean
Reminders stop only what they name: the say–do gap is narrow salience, and generic
reminders have hard limits.
Start-of-task reminders work nearly as well: timing matters less than content, which
is good news for practical guardrails.
Own judgement matches or beats the reminder at scale: self-knowledge does help,
which would revise the sprint's "salience, not self" claim.
Support S2: when recognition appears while acting
This is the mechanism check behind the in-context results: does the model represent that
a step is wrong before it says so, or only once the words appear?
What we have. A probe that spots violations when the model judges a run (AUC 0.95)
couldn't separate violating from clean runs while it acts (0.55). A probe fitted on acting
runs only read the violation at or after the model first mentioned it.
Why it is still open. Recent work finds that agents' knowledge of the task stays readable
inside them even while their actions go wrong. Reasoning improves the match between
belief and action, and editing activations gave limited control (The Belief–Action Gap).
Benchmarks also show models recognise their own violations afterwards in 61–96% of
runs. Nobody has made a timing claim about when, inside the run, the recognition
appears.
What runs. Fit the probe on acting runs, cross-validated by scenario, with at least 5 runs
per scenario. Then read it turn by turn up to the first mention.
Fix from the frontier check: add runs where the model mentions the rule without
breaking it. Otherwise the probe might just be tracking the words about the rule, not
recognition of a violation.

The signal appears only when the words do: recognition while acting runs through
putting it into words. That fits "knowledge activates when verbalised", and it explains
why written forecasts and reminders work.
The signal appears earlier: the model represents the violation but doesn't act on it, a
gap of use, not of knowledge.
Results to present as replications
These sprint findings match published work closely, so on the slides they are "consistent
with prior work", not discoveries. They still carry the story; they just aren't claimed as new.
Two sprint findings looked new in this check and stay as findings: procedural habits failing
to reach self-report while broad traits do (already present in OLMo's base model), and the
asymmetric probe timing.

confirmation of forecast-then-act against its strongest control, A0.1 at full power, and
Main bet B's pressure gate plus capping. If Main bet A stays at one seed, it is presented as
a pilot. The support checks are upside.

the model's own forecast-then-act runs. Pilot the authority pressure gate from Petri
seeds, and the ODCV scenarios that allow several violation types for A0.1.
2. Thursday early: Main bet A's training pilots start (four versions, one seed each). A0.1's
powered ODCV runs start. A0.2 and A0.3 run on the 14B justice desk. Main bet B's
capping runs if the gate passed.
